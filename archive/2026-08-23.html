🌍 World
  • The US–Canada trade war is real — New US tariffs of 50% on a wide swath of Canadian goods took effect early Saturday, minutes after Prime Minister Mark Carney suspended trade negotiations rather than accept what he called "last-minute changes" to the American terms that were "unfair" and "uneconomic." Canada will match them dollar for dollar, with retaliatory duties on roughly $28 billion of US steel, electronics and other products starting Sept. 8. Carney called the American move "a miscalculation" and said the response is meant to protect Canadian workers, farmers and businesses; the two countries are each other's largest trading partners. (CNBC, NBC News, CTV News)
  • Putin threatens Ukraine's economy — After a Saturday in which Russian attacks killed at least seven people in Ukraine and Ukrainian strikes on Russia and occupied territory killed at least ten, Vladimir Putin warned of "far more destructive" strikes on Ukrainian economic infrastructure and said Kyiv had opened a "Pandora's box." Ukrainian drones hit a warehouse belonging to Ozon, Russia's second-largest online marketplace, in Samara Oblast, forcing more than 500 employees out; a Russian strike on a DTEK gas production facility in the Poltava region suspended operations there. The escalation came a day after Moscow said it was open to "new ideas" on ending the war, provided they match Putin's existing goals. (Al Jazeera)
  • Israel opens bids on E1 — Israel's Housing Ministry published a tender on Aug. 18 and immediately opened bidding for more than 1,200 homes in E1, the roughly 12-square-kilometre tract east of Jerusalem between the city and the settlement of Maale Adumim, part of a plan for about 3,400 homes green-lit last year. Building there would separate East Jerusalem from the West Bank and sever its northern and southern halves, which is why the UN and 21 countries including Britain and France have called it a violation of international law; European leaders demanded Israel halt the project, and the Palestinian ambassador to the UN called the move "extremely dangerous, irresponsible, and threatening." (CNN, Euronews)
🇺🇸 US & Politics
  • Hawk Fire forces 42,000 from Reno — A brush fire that started Saturday on a hillside near Peavine Mountain northwest of Reno had burned 15,040 acres across Washoe and Sierra counties by Sunday with zero containment and about 400 firefighters on it. Roughly 42,000 people covering 13,700 residences are under evacuation orders and another 45,000 are under warnings, with the "GO NOW" zone reaching the edge of the University of Nevada campus and about 50,000 customers losing power in Reno and Sparks. Gov. Joe Lombardo declared a state of emergency in Washoe County and mobilized the National Guard — two helicopter crews for aerial firefighting and 60 troops to guard evacuated neighborhoods. A preliminary investigation found the fire was human-caused; structures have been lost, but assessment teams have not yet been able to get in. (Washington Post, CNN, KOLO)
  • Court asked to stop the Kennedy Center inscription — Rep. Joyce Beatty, an Ohio Democrat and ex-officio member of the Kennedy Center board, filed an emergency request asking US District Judge Christopher Cooper to block the center from carving Trump's name into the building — the follow-through on the board's vote earlier this month to rename it. Her lawyers argue that only Congress can rename the complex, which by congressional mandate is a memorial to President Kennedy. The center said in separate filings that it will not put the name on the facade before Sept. 8; if the plan proceeds in full, Trump's name would appear twice on the entryway sign and in a third location on the grounds. (Washington Post, NPR, Forbes)
  • Colleges sue over the four-year visa cap — The Presidents' Alliance, representing close to 600 public and private colleges, joined by the United Auto Workers and the American Federation of Teachers, sued the Department of Homeland Security in federal court in Massachusetts to vacate a rule capping F-1 and J-1 student, exchange-visitor and journalist visas at four years. The rule, finalized in July and effective Sept. 15, replaces the longstanding "duration of status" standard under which a visa lasted as long as the program did; students who need more than four years would have to apply for an extension. Plaintiffs call it arbitrary and capricious and warn it lands hardest on doctoral students and postdocs whose research runs longer. (Courthouse News, Higher Ed Dive, STAT)
⚡ Tech & AI
  • Apple cuts 200 from Siri and Vision Pro — Apple eliminated about 200 roles, split roughly evenly between the Vision Pro division — concentrated in the gaming and immersive video teams — and the Siri organization plus a group called Intelligent Systems Experience that handles AI integration across Apple's devices. The Siri cuts hit staff who worked on the pre-LLM assistant, superseded by the language-model Siri that shipped with iOS 27 and which needs different expertise. Reporting indicates hardware attention is shifting from Vision Pro toward lighter smart glasses that would not support immersive video or gaming at all. Apple said affected employees will get support and can apply for other roles internally. (Bloomberg, 9to5Mac, TechCrunch)
  • Memory costs push AI servers up 15% — Nvidia has warned its largest customers that prices for AI servers built around its chips will rise more than 15% in many configurations, applying to systems shipped in early 2027 and varying by chip generation and memory loadout, including the Grace Blackwell and Vera Rubin platforms. The driver is memory, not silicon: contract prices for server DRAM jumped 53% to 58% in the second quarter from the previous quarter as supply tightened at Samsung and SK hynix. Contract manufacturers that assemble servers for Microsoft, Google and Oracle have already passed the notice along. (Korea JoongAng Daily, The Next Web)
  • Tesla ends the Solar Roof — Tesla told its certified third-party installers it will stop supplying Solar Roof tiles and will send conventional solar panels instead, having concluded internally that the tile product was not financially viable. The Solar Roof page now redirects to the solar panels page and the product has been removed from the Energy navigation menu. That ends the line about ten years after Elon Musk unveiled it in 2016, weeks before shareholders approved Tesla's roughly $2.6 billion acquisition of SolarCity; the company had quietly stopped reporting Solar Roof deployment figures in early 2024. (Electrek, TechCrunch, Gizmodo)

🌊 San Diego
  • Arizona eyes the Sea of Cortez — Two desalination companies, Canada's EPCOR Water Innovation Partners and Spain's Acciona, have pitched Arizona's Water Infrastructure Financing Authority on plants that would pull seawater from the Sea of Cortez off Baja California to offset cuts to Arizona's Colorado River allocation. The EPCOR proposal would be twice the size of the largest plant of its kind, drawing 1.2 billion gallons of seawater a day to produce 446 million gallons of fresh water. The mechanism is a "paper water" exchange — the same structure the San Diego County Water Authority is pursuing to sell its expensive Carlsbad desalinated supply to Arizona and Nevada rather than keep paying for water the region does not need. (Voice of San Diego)
  • City fills potholes nobody reported — San Diego crews have repaired 1,131 previously unreported potholes since mid-May under a program that sends teams into neighborhoods that historically file fewer Get It Done requests, on the theory that low complaint volume reflects underreporting rather than good pavement. Crews rotate weekly among Council Districts 4, 8 and 9 and deploy whenever the citywide backlog drops below 100 open requests — which it has in 13 of the past 14 weeks. The push is timed ahead of the rainy season, when water in existing cracks accelerates the damage. (Inside San Diego, CBS 8)
  • 12,000 at the book festival — More than 12,000 people came to the second annual KPBS San Diego Book Festival on Saturday at the University of San Diego, about 4,000 more than last year, including an estimated 4,200 children. KPBS used the day to announce the 2026 One Book, One San Diego selections, marking the 20th anniversary of the regionwide shared-reading program, alongside seven live performances of music, poetry and spoken word. (KPBS)

Crews could not reach it from the top of Anahulu and worked in from the corner of the two roads, with the Hanalei, Kaiakea, Kapaʻa and Līhuʻe stations, three air units and police responding. (The Garden Island, Hawaii News Now)

🧬 Mito Research
  • The first approvals arrive — none for MELAS yet — A structured scoping review and horizon scan of every primary mitochondrial disease trial and regulatory decision from 2000 through November 2025, led by Amel Karaa at Massachusetts General Hospital with the United Mitochondrial Disease Foundation, Mito Foundation, Lily Foundation and POLG Foundation as co-authors, documents the point at which the field crossed from no disease-modifying approvals to several. The EMA cleared idebenone for Leber hereditary optic neuropathy in 2015; Friedreich's ataxia drew EMA and FDA approvals in 2023; and in 2025 the FDA approved treatments for Barth syndrome and for TK2 deficiency — the first US approvals in primary mitochondrial disease. The authors attribute the shift to precision approaches, naming gene therapy and nucleoside therapy specifically, paired with natural-history-informed trial designs and better outcome measures, after two decades of broad antioxidant and metabolic trials that mostly failed or returned mixed results. None of these therapies applies to m.3243A>G, and the roadmap is candid that sustaining the momentum depends on sensitive biomarkers and natural-history infrastructure the field still largely lacks. What is new and worth holding onto is narrower but real: regulators have now accepted evidence packages built for ultra-rare mitochondrial disorders, which was an open question a year ago. (Ther Adv Rare Dis, via PubMed · DOI)
