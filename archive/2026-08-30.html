🌍 World
  • The Nepal–Tibet flood toll passes 750 — Four days after the glacier-and-rock collapse covered here Thursday, officials put the confirmed dead at 750 on Sunday and the missing at more than 3,000. Nepal accounts for 734 of the deaths, with 2,498 missing and 7,514 people rescued; China reported 16 dead in Gyirong County and 546 missing. Among those unaccounted for on the Tibetan side are 261 foreign nationals from 23 countries, including 18 Americans, 49 Indians, 33 Latvians and 15 Britons. Nepali authorities issued fresh warnings on Sunday as water levels rose again in the Bhotekoshi River, telling residents of the affected districts to stay on alert. (ABC News, CNN)
  • A Russian drone hits an ammunition depot near Kyiv — A drone struck a facility storing explosives in the village of Myla, about five kilometres west of Kyiv, around 8:10 p.m. on Aug. 28, setting a fire that touched off waves of secondary detonations. At least 38 people were killed. President Zelenskyy blamed “appalling negligence by those who stored explosive materials right next to where people were living,” and Ukrainian prosecutors opened a criminal investigation into the storage decision rather than only the strike. The blast came inside a sustained bombardment: Russia fired close to 2,000 attack drones, more than 1,600 glide bombs and 31 missiles at Ukraine over the past week, and Kyiv entered a fourth consecutive day of drone attacks on Saturday, when a strike killed a toddler in the Obolon district. (Al Jazeera, CNN)
  • Iceland votes against reopening EU talks — Icelanders rejected restarting European Union accession negotiations by 52.8% to 47.2% in Saturday’s referendum, with 225,031 ballots cast and turnout at 82.5%. The question was not membership itself but whether the government could reopen the talks it suspended in 2013; a “yes” would have sent any eventual deal back to voters in a second referendum. The government had argued that renewed negotiations would help a country of roughly 400,000 people manage economic exposure and Arctic security pressure, while opponents campaigned on fishing rights and sovereignty. The result shelves the question for at least two more years. (Euronews, Al Jazeera, ABC News Australia)
  • Google splits the name of Lake Ontario — Google said it will show the lake as “Lake America” to users in the United States, keep “Lake Ontario” for users in Canada, and display both names everywhere else — applying its standing policy for water bodies whose names differ by country to President Trump’s renaming decree. Trump said he was renaming the lake because trade talks with Canada had gone badly; those talks collapsed last week and he announced a 50% tariff on a broad range of Canadian imports, which Ottawa matched. Ontario Premier Doug Ford answered on Saturday with a large bilingual billboard on the Canadian shoreline near the border reading “Lake Ontario — Now and Always.” (NPR, Al Jazeera)
🇺🇸 US & Politics
  • Dolly Parton is buried in Nashville — Parton died on Aug. 25 at 80, after what her team described as a brief illness with cancer, and was laid to rest this weekend in a private service at Woodlawn Memorial Park, kept to about a dozen immediate family members in keeping with her wishes. Her niece Lainey Mae Parton confirmed the funeral publicly on Saturday, saying she had waited until her grandmother was buried. Vice President JD Vance gives the keynote on the first night and President Trump appears both nights, keynoting the second; the RNC says it will be its largest convention in a decade and is releasing some free tickets to the public. It lands ten weeks from the election and shortly after the Cook Political Report moved the open Texas and Iowa Senate races from Lean Republican to Toss Up, putting six seats in that column, five of them Republican-held. ⚡ Tech & AI
  • A judge voids the Pentagon’s Anthropic blacklist — US District Judge Rita Lin in Northern California ruled on Friday that the Defense Department’s designation of Anthropic as a supply-chain risk violated the First Amendment and the Fifth Amendment’s due-process clause, and ordered the government to rescind the directives issued against the company. In a 59-page opinion she found the government’s rationale rested principally on Anthropic’s public criticism of the administration’s AI positions, writing that “the empty invocation of national security is not a blank check to punish and retaliate against government critics.” The designation grew out of a $200 million contract dispute over how the Pentagon could run Claude on classified systems, and was the first time a US company had been publicly named a supply-chain risk under the procurement statute involved. The government is expected to appeal. (NBC News, CNBC, Forbes)
  • Music publishers sue Anthropic over lyrics — Thirty-five publishers, including affiliates of Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell Music, filed suit on Friday in the Northern District of California against Anthropic, chief executive Dario Amodei and co-founder Benjamin Mann, alleging the company torrented, scraped and downloaded copyrighted works at scale to train Claude and then reproduced lyrics as model output. Songs named in the complaint include “Eye of the Tiger,” “Ain’t No Mountain High Enough” and Taylor Swift’s “Paper Rings.” The publishers want a jury trial and up to $150,000 per infringed work, plus $25,000 for each instance of stripped copyright management information. With this filing, the publishing arms of all three major music companies are now litigating against Anthropic. (TechCrunch, Axios, Engadget)
  • Tim Cook’s last day as Apple CEO is Monday — The handover announced in April takes effect this week: Cook’s final day as chief executive is Aug. 31, and John Ternus becomes CEO and joins the board on Sept. 1, with Cook moving to executive chairman. Apple held a farewell for Cook at the Caffé Macs cafeteria on campus last week — roughly 200 people, a OneRepublic set, and tributes from Laurene Powell Jobs, former COO Jeff Williams, services chief Eddy Cue and Ternus himself. Ternus, 50, joined Apple in 2001 and has run hardware engineering since 2021; the “Surprise and shine” event on Sept. 9, where the iPhone 18 Pro line and the foldable are expected, will be his first product launch as chief executive. (MacRumors, AppleInsider)
  • Nvidia doubles revenue and guides higher — Nvidia reported fiscal second-quarter revenue of $96.2 billion on Wednesday, more than double a year earlier and ahead of the roughly $92 billion expected, with data-centre revenue up 117% to a company-record $89.02 billion on the ramp of Blackwell Ultra. Adjusted earnings were $2.22 a share against $2.10 expected, and gross margin held at 75%, though the company said rising memory costs will pull that down next quarter. Guidance was the surprise: about $108 billion for the third quarter assuming no revenue from China, and Jensen Huang put calendar-2027 growth near 70%, roughly twice what analysts had modelled. (CNBC)

🌊 San Diego
  • The city settles the La Jolla sinkhole for $10 million — The City Council gave final approval to a $10 million payout to the La Jolla Terrace Condominiums homeowners association and one unit owner, closing claims filed in 2024 over a drainpipe failure that opened a hole more than 80 feet by 40 feet steps from the buildings. The ground movement cracked foundations and shifted whole units, forcing residents out. The settlement does not end the matter: a separate suit brought by other condominium owners and some renters is still active. (Times of San Diego, CBS 8)
  • And $6.5 million over a pothole death — On the same agenda, the council approved $6.5 million to the widow of James Wadsworth, a 60-year-old neurophysiology specialist at UC San Diego Health who was riding a scooter to the Sorrento Valley Coaster station in January 2023 when he hit a large pothole on Roselle Street, was thrown, and died of his injuries. His family sued in September 2023 arguing the city had failed to maintain the roadway; the council agreed terms in closed session in July after nearly three years of litigation. Two street-condition payouts totalling $16.5 million cleared in one sitting. (Times of San Diego)

🔋 Electric Vehicles
  • VW’s electric California camper gets real numbers — The ID. California Cruise previewed here on Aug. 20 was actually unveiled Friday at Caravan Salon in Düsseldorf, and the specifications landed with it. It is built on the ID. Buzz Pro with a 79 or 86 kWh pack, sleeps two, and leans on vehicle-to-load: 2,000 watts continuous and 3.6 kW peak, enough to run a kettle, a laptop or camp kitchen gear, or charge an e-bike. A dedicated “California Mode” is rated to keep the camper systems running for up to 48 hours. Pricing starts around €60,800 (about $70,800) in Germany with first deliveries in early 2027 — and it is a European car for now, not offered in the US. VW says it is the first of an electric California family expanding through 2028. (Electrek, electrive, Carscoops)
  • Kia recalls 21,290 EV9s over a child-seat airbag fault — The recall covers 2025 and certain 2026 EV9s built at Kia’s Georgia plant. A supplier misrouted the bladder tube in the Occupant Detection System mat, and the tube can become pinched after the front passenger seat has been adjusted several times — leaving the system unable to detect a child or child restraint and switch the passenger airbag off. Dealers will inspect and, where needed, replace the mat and tube and reroute the components, free of charge. Owner letters go out Oct. 20, and affected VINs should be searchable on the NHTSA recall site from Sept. 4. Worth a check for anyone running a three-row EV with a car seat up front. (Electrek)
