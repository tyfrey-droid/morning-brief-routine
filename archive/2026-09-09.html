🧬 Mito Research
  • Gene therapy restores sight in an mtDNA mouse — A team including Douglas Wallace at Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia and Min-Xin Guan reported Tuesday in JCI Insight that they moved a mitochondrial gene into the nucleus and made it work in a living animal. The approach, allotopic expression, recodes a mitochondrial gene for the nuclear genome and adds a targeting sequence so the protein is delivered back into the mitochondrion; here an AAV2 vector carried a recoded ND6 into mice bearing the homoplasmic ND6P25L mutation, a model of Leber hereditary optic neuropathy. Expression held in the retina and optic nerve with no apparent systemic toxicity, and treating before symptoms slowed disease, reduced retinal and optic-nerve damage and restored visual function. The honest limit for a MELAS family: this works because ND6 codes for a protein, which can be rewritten in nuclear language. The m.3243A>G variant sits in a transfer-RNA gene, which this strategy cannot address directly — and it is a mouse. What it establishes is that the whole allotopic approach survives contact with a living animal, which had not been shown. (Via PubMed · DOI)
  • The first pooled numbers on L-arginine — L-arginine is standard care for MELAS stroke-like episodes, given largely on mechanism and small studies. In Therapeutic Advances in Rare Disease on Sept. 4, a group led from the same CHOP mitochondrial programme pooled the human evidence for the first time: seven studies, 76 patients with mitochondrial disease against 81 controls, ages 8 to 63. Across every measure the vessels were impaired — reduced flow-mediated dilation, reactive hyperaemia and leg-movement hyperaemia, less nitric-oxide synthesis, and heart-muscle PET showing reduced oxidative rate constants despite normal perfusion. Giving a nitric-oxide precursor, L-arginine or L-citrulline, improved endothelial reactivity and produced modest gains in myocardial oxidative metabolism, which the authors read as a partly reversible nitric-oxide-deficiency endotheliopathy. Caveats are real: seven non-randomised studies, small numbers, mostly adults. But it is the first quantified answer to whether the drug does what it is given for. (Via PubMed · DOI)
