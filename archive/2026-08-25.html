🌍 World
  • Washington opens an economic front on Iran — Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced Monday what he called an "economic D-Day": an expansion of US secondary sanctions on Iran into shipping, oil, digital assets, gold and aviation, aimed at pushing Iranian crude exports from roughly 1.5 million barrels a day toward zero. Secondary sanctions force third parties to pick a side — a Chinese bank or a Malaysian refiner can do business with Iran or keep access to the dollar system, not both — and Bessent said violators would get only a short "cure period." Tehran answered by threatening to halt oil exports from the Persian Gulf and warning it would treat cooperation with the sanctions as an act of war; the rial hit a record low against the dollar and the IMF projects Iran's economy shrinking nearly 5.5% this year. (Axios, CBS News, NBC News)
  • Canada matches 50% with 50% — Prime Minister Mark Carney announced retaliatory tariffs Tuesday on about $20 billion of American goods, doubling Canada's counter-duties on US steel and aluminum to 50% and adding 50% levies on American dairy, furniture, clothing, video-game consoles, smartphones and other electronics — reaching past industrial inputs into ordinary shopping. It answers the 50% US tariffs that took effect Saturday after trade talks collapsed; Carney said Washington's demands during those talks showed it wanted to "destroy our major industries," and the Trump administration is already weighing further trade measures against Canada. (Bloomberg, PBS NewsHour, The Hill)
  • China's biggest recall is about door handles — Tesla and eight Chinese automakers — Geely, Dongfeng, Xiaomi, Chery, BAIC, FAW, Leapmotor and Xpeng — are recalling roughly 4.3 million vehicles in China, the country's largest automotive recall, over retractable door handles that can be hard to find or operate in an emergency. Tesla's share is about 2.98 million cars. Remedies range from software updates to warning labels and clearer markings around the handles, with Tesla also pushing an update that opens windows automatically after a crash. Regulators moved after repeated cases in which drivers, passengers and rescuers struggled with hidden handles during vehicle fires. (The Japan Times, The National)
🇺🇸 US & Politics
  • Supreme Court clears part of the mail-voting order — The justices voted 6-3 on Monday, along ideological lines, to lift a Massachusetts judge's June injunction that had blocked key parts of Trump's executive order restricting mail-in balloting from taking effect in California and 22 other states that sued. The order directs the creation of a federal list of eligible voters and instructs the Postal Service to deliver ballots only to people on that list. The ruling is procedural and temporary rather than a decision on the merits, and a separate injunction still blocks another central piece of the plan — but it lands with less than 12 weeks to the November midterms. (Washington Post, NPR, Votebeat)
  • The largest mass visa revocation on record — The State Department is preparing to cancel the B-1 business and B-2 tourist visas of up to 200,000 foreign nationals who entered the US between 2016 and 2026 and later applied for asylum. Officials say the revocations would not automatically trigger deportation: most people with pending asylum cases would shift into a different immigration category while those cases proceed, stripping the visitor designation rather than the claim. State is coordinating with the Department of Homeland Security and the announcement is expected within weeks. If it goes ahead it would be the largest single mass visa revocation in US history. (NPR, Al Jazeera, Axios)
  • A $103,265 fee to hire an H-1B worker — DHS published a proposed rule in the Federal Register on Tuesday that would charge employers $103,265 for every cap-subject H-1B petition, on top of all existing fees, with a 30-day public comment period before any final rule. Cap-exempt filers — universities, affiliated nonprofits and nonprofit or government research organizations — would be excluded. This is the administration's second route to the same destination: a federal district court in Massachusetts vacated the $100,000 fee imposed by presidential proclamation back in June, and the First Circuit declined to reinstate it while the appeal proceeds. (USCIS, Forbes)
⚡ Tech & AI
  • Apple's Mac mini gets the M6 — Apple announced a new Mac mini on Tuesday built around an all-new M6 chip — a 12-core CPU and 12-core GPU, up from 10 and 10 in the M5, and the first Apple silicon with two 16-core Neural Engines instead of one — or the M5 Pro released earlier this year. Against the outgoing M4 model Apple claims up to 40% faster CPU, roughly 2× graphics, 2× storage speed and 4× AI performance. The M6 configuration starts at $899 and the M5 Pro at $1,699; pre-orders opened Tuesday with machines arriving in stores Sept. 22, alongside a refreshed Mac Studio and the new M6 and M5 Ultra chips. (Apple, MacRumors, Bloomberg)
  • Nine indicted over Nvidia servers routed to China — Taiwan's Keelung District Prosecutors' Office charged nine people on Monday over the alleged illegal resale of Supermicro AI servers fitted with Nvidia's B300 GPUs. The defendants include a distribution manager at Nvidia's Taiwan office, two sales managers at Supermicro's Taiwan branch and the chief executive of distributor Albatron Technology. Prosecutors say false end-user paperwork claimed 130 servers would be installed at a rented facility in Taiwan; 74 instead reached Chinese customers, shipped directly or transshipped through Indonesia, Japan and Hong Kong, with a shell company in Japan moving eight more. Eight of the nine face breach-of-trust and document-forgery charges. (Washington Post, PBS NewsHour, Bloomberg)
  • The iPhone event is expected Sept. 9 — Bloomberg's Mark Gurman reports Apple will send media invitations this week for its annual fall event, most likely landing on Wednesday, Sept. 9. Expected on stage: the iPhone 18 Pro and 18 Pro Max, a foldable model reported to be called iPhone Ultra with a 5.5-inch outer and roughly 7.8-inch inner display and Touch ID in the side button, Apple Watch Series 12 with a returning ceramic case option, and two AirPods 5 models. It would be the first major launch under John Ternus, who becomes CEO on Sept. 1. Nothing is official yet — this is reporting, not an Apple announcement. (MacRumors, 9to5Mac)

Freeway driving is part of the plan. KPBS's framing — "like it or not" — reflects that the remaining questions are about timing and public reaction rather than permission. (KPBS, NBC 7 San Diego)

  • A pixel you can reprogram mid-shoot — Y.M.Cinema writes up a paper in Sensors describing a "programmable readout" (PRO) pixel: a sensor design in which a single imager can be reconfigured to prioritize low noise, high frame rate or high dynamic range, instead of having one of those permanently optimized at manufacture and the others traded away. This is research rather than product — nothing is shipping and nothing here bolts to an FX6 — but it targets exactly the tradeoff every cinema sensor currently bakes in at the factory. 🔋 Electric Vehicles
  • PG&E pays more EVs to feed the grid — PG&E expanded its vehicle-to-everything program on Monday, adding the Kia EV9 and EV6, the previous-generation Nissan Leaf, Volvo EX90, Polestar 3, the new Chevrolet Bolt EV and Cadillac's Celestiq and Escalade IQL to the eligible-vehicle list, along with Bidirectional Energy and PowerFlex as approved partners. Qualifying owners can back up their homes and send stored battery energy back to the grid during peak hours and be paid for it; enrollment runs through June 30, 2027. The family-format entries are the relevant ones here — a three-row EV9 or EX90 is a house-sized battery on wheels — with the caveat that this is PG&E's Northern and Central California territory, not SDG&E's. (PG&E, InsideEVs)
