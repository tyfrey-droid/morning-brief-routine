🌍 World
  • The Gulf turns on Tehran — The United Arab Emirates, historically Iran's second-largest commercial partner, suspended all trade and financial transactions with Tehran after two ballistic missiles were fired toward it Tuesday night, triggering the first nationwide shelter warnings in weeks. Iran called the accusation "unfounded" and warned of "false-flag operations." The cut is substantial: the UAE supplied more than 30% of Iran's imports (about $21 billion) and took roughly 13% of its exports, and has served as the re-export hub that absorbed much of the sanctions shock. Trump vowed "tremendous economic consequences" for any country still doing business with Iran, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent described the plan as economic isolation plus a continued blockade of Iranian ports. (CNN, NBC News, Washington Post)
  • Evergrande's founder gets life — A Shenzhen court sentenced Hui Ka Yan, 67, to life in prison for financial fraud and bribery, confiscating all his personal property and stripping his political rights permanently. The court found he inflated Evergrande's assets, concealed its liabilities and misappropriated company funds; he pleaded guilty in April. Evergrande was fined 8.82 billion yuan (about $1.31 billion) and its onshore unit Hengda Real Estate 7 billion yuan — among the largest corporate criminal fines a Chinese court has levied. Hui's two sons and more than 50 others drew terms from 22 months to 18 years. The developer collapsed with over $300 billion in liabilities, the trigger for China's property crisis. (NPR, CNN, South China Morning Post)
  • Israel opens its first Gaza probes — Israel's military announced its first criminal investigations into troop conduct in Gaza, covering the killings of 5-year-old Hind Rajab and her family and of 15 Red Crescent and Civil Defense paramedics in Rafah — a case in which soldiers fired on an ambulance, then on the vehicles sent to look for it, and bulldozed the bodies into a mass grave. The military said it had completed reviews of 150 incidents and reached decisions on five. It declined to investigate three strikes that killed World Central Kitchen and Doctors Without Borders aid workers; WCK founder José Andrés called that decision "wrong and painful." (NPR, Washington Post, NBC News)
🇺🇸 US & Politics
  • National debt passes $40 trillion — Treasury data released Wednesday put the gross national debt at $40,047,425,768,420.22 as of Aug. 18 — five months after it crossed $39 trillion in March, and roughly double where it stood less than a decade ago. Interest is the part that bites: the government paid $970 billion on the debt in fiscal 2025 against $916 billion on national defense, will clear $1 trillion in interest this year, and now spends more servicing debt than on anything except Social Security. Debt held by the public has also passed annual GDP for the first time in about 80 years. (NPR, The Hill, Washington Post)
  • Trump picks Overton for the FDA — Trump said he will nominate Dr. Heidi Overton, currently a deputy assistant to the president for domestic policy, as FDA commissioner. She completed a general surgery residency at Johns Hopkins and was a White House fellow in his first term. Confirmation is not assured in either party: Sen. Patty Murray (D-Wash.) said she will vote no, calling Overton unqualified for the job, and Sen. Bill Cassidy (R-La.), a gastroenterologist who chairs the HELP Committee, wrote that her "lack of managerial experience does not prepare her well for leading a large organization that is already dealing with staffing and morale issues." (NPR, CBS News, PBS NewsHour, Axios)
⚡ Tech & AI
  • Google buys Spirit's data trove — Google won a bankruptcy auction for the business data of Spirit Airlines, which shut down earlier this year, bidding $10 million against $7.5 million from AI recruiting firm Mercor. The haul is roughly 100 million emails, 500 million Microsoft Teams chats, 7.2 billion competitor flight-pricing records, 7.5 billion passenger transaction records going back to 2008 and 175,000 employee records dating to 1986 — all of it to train AI models. Google does not get the 97.5 million passenger profiles or the 50.2 million Free Spirit loyalty records, and says a third party will scrub personally identifiable information before delivery. A bankruptcy judge still has to approve. (CNN, Forbes, Tom's Hardware)
  • ChatGPT ads reach Europe — OpenAI said Wednesday it will start serving ads inside ChatGPT across 31 European markets on Aug. 24 — Germany, France, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands, the Nordics, Ireland, Poland and more — its largest geographic expansion of the ad business so far. Ads go only to adults on the Free and Go tiers; Plus, Pro, Business, Enterprise and Edu users see none. Buying runs through OpenAI's ad-solutions team and agency partners at first, with self-service coming later. OpenAI reported ChatGPT Search averaged 159.1 million monthly active users in the EU over the six months ending March 2026. (OpenAI, Adweek, Digiday)
  • OpenAI locks out vetted hackers — Five security researchers told TechCrunch their access to OpenAI's Trusted Access for Cyber program — which gives vetted defenders relaxed guardrails so they can find and report vulnerabilities faster — was revoked without warning, including at the top "Daybreak Blue" tier. OpenAI called it a technical error affecting a limited number of users, mostly researchers based outside the US and Europe, and asked them to reapply and re-verify; several found their accounts marked ineligible and could not restart the process. (TechCrunch, The Register)

🌊 San Diego
  • Fairgrounds board kills Del Mar housing deal — The 22nd District Agricultural Association, the governor-appointed board that runs the Del Mar Fairgrounds, voted 5-3 Tuesday to terminate the exclusive negotiating agreement it signed with the city of Del Mar in 2024, ending two years of talks about building affordable housing on fairgrounds land. The pressure now shifts back to Del Mar, which has repeatedly rejected housing projects of its own: the only site the board considered viable is an RV park that sits on state fairgrounds land but falls inside San Diego city limits, so Del Mar would need San Diego to annex it before the units could count toward Del Mar's state housing obligation. (Voice of San Diego)
  • Housing groups sue over a sidewalk rule — Californians for Homeownership and the California Housing Defense Fund sued San Diego, San Francisco and Montebello over how they are applying SB 79, the 2025 law letting developers exceed local zoning near major transit stops. San Diego's reading requires a project to be linked to a bus or trolley station by a dedicated sidewalk to qualify; the plaintiffs say the statute asks only for a "safe walkway," and that the city is using small sidewalk gaps and shared alleyways to disqualify sites within blocks of Trolley stations. (Times of San Diego)
  • Darth Vader speaks on Flock — A man in a full Darth Vader costume addressed the City Council's Public Safety and Livable Neighborhoods Committee on Wednesday, telling members "the emperor is a fan of Flock" and that the automated license plate readers should be kept so the city can "surveil the rebel scum as they move from playground to playground." The bit was opposition, not endorsement — a protest against a network the council voted 6-3 in December to keep using over hundreds of residents' objections, many of them worried the plate data could be pulled into federal immigration enforcement. (The Hill, 404 Media, Axios)
🌺 Kauaʻi
  • Another system forms behind Lala — Tropical Depression Two-C formed Thursday about 900 miles east-southeast of Hilo with 35 mph sustained winds, moving west-northwest near 15 mph and expected to strengthen into Tropical Storm Moke. Forecasters have it passing near or south of the Big Island this weekend and staying mostly south of the chain, so Kauaʻi is on the favorable side of the track — but the Big Island, still recovering from Lala, faces the flooding-rain and wind threat again. (Hawaii News Now, Maui Now)
  • Island water tests come back mostly clean — Surfrider Kauaʻi's Blue Water Task Force reported that only nine of its sampling sites around the island showed enterococcus bacteria above state water-quality standards this month, and every one of those was at or near a freshwater outlet — the usual pattern after heavy rain. Separately, the county began an 8-to-10-week inspection and condition assessment of the Wailua–Kapaʻa sewer collection system on Aug. 19. (Kauai Now)
