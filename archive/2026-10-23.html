🧬 Mito Research

  • "Hypoxia in a pill," now working after symptoms start — Chronic low oxygen has been known for years to rescue mouse models of mitochondrial disease, but breathing 11 per cent oxygen continuously is not a treatment anyone can live on. Vamsi Mootha's lab at Mass General, HHMI and the Broad has been trying to reproduce the effect with two drugs instead: a haemoglobin affinity enhancer that limits how much oxygen the blood releases, plus a HIF-2α inhibitor that blocks the body's compensating surge in red-cell production. Their first attempt helped but fell short of real hypoxia. This version swaps in a longer-acting second-generation haemoglobin compound, and the numbers moved: started in mice with already advanced complex I deficiency at 50 days old, the single drug lifted median survival from 62 to 105 days and the pair to 158. The same combination halted motor decline in a Friedreich's ataxia model and reversed it in a Parkinson's model, with no weight loss or signs of pulmonary hypertension. What makes this worth reading for a family already living with established disease is the timing: the benefit did not require catching the disease early. The caveats are real — these are mice, the model is nuclear-gene complex I deficiency rather than m.3243A>G, and no human trial of this combination exists. But both drug classes come from compounds already tested in people for other conditions, which is the shortest path a mitochondrial therapy can take. (Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A — doi.org, PubMed)

Indexed in PubMed; published October 9 in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. Academic funding (HHMI, Mass General, Harvard Medical School, Broad Institute, UAB), not industry-sponsored.
