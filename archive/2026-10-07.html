🌍 World
  • France suspends high school classes — Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu has suspended high school classes nationwide for the rest of the week as the student-led protests over education funding escalated sharply. More than a quarter of a million people rallied Monday, by the government’s own count, and the Justice Ministry now puts arrests above 5,060 — 87% of them minors. More than 170 pupils, 78 school staff and 600 police officers have been injured since the protests began; a 15-year-old in Lens lost a hand to a police grenade. The students’ demands are concrete rather than ideological: cover absent teachers, lengthen lunch breaks, shorten the school day. (NPR, Al Jazeera, CNBC)
  • US pulls its bombers out of a UK base — The Air Force abruptly flew roughly a dozen B-1 bombers home from RAF Fairford in England on Sunday after intelligence — passed on by Israel — pointed to a “potentially complex” drone attack on the base ordered by Iran’s Revolutionary Guard Corps. The base went into lockdown and families were moved; British police arrested a 22-year-old man in London on suspicion of preparing terrorist acts, and a second arrest followed Tuesday. The Pentagon says every bomber deployed to Fairford is now back at its home station. Iran denies any involvement in the alleged plot. (Axios, CNN, PBS NewsHour)

Black — its first port call since leaving Norfolk in March, with more than 4,500 sailors and Marines going ashore.   • Germany arrests its former spy chief — August Hanning, who ran the BND foreign intelligence service from 1998 to 2005, was arrested Tuesday at his home in Nordwalde, near Münster, on suspicion of espionage and attempted treason. A second former senior BND official was detained in Bavaria. Prosecutors allege that from 2010 a serving officer passed Hanning roughly 2,000 internal documents, many classified, in exchange for payment. They have not said which foreign intelligence service he is alleged to have been working for. (NPR, CNN, ABC News Australia)
  • Two Nobels for seeing the invisible — The physics prize went Tuesday to Francis Halzen of the University of Wisconsin–Madison, who realised nearly four decades ago that the ice at the South Pole could be turned into a telescope: the IceCube observatory he built threads 5,160 light sensors through a cubic kilometre of Antarctic ice and found the first high-energy neutrinos from deep space. The chemistry prize was announced this morning for Henri B. Kagan, 95, of Université Paris-Sud and Kenso Soai, 76, of Tokyo University of Science, for explaining how molecules that are mirror images of each other can be made to favour one hand over the other. (NBC News, NPR, CNN)

⚡ Tech & AI
  • OpenAI drops 372 math results at once — The company posted hundreds of AI-written papers covering 372 results, including a claimed proof of the four-dimensional Kakeya conjecture and progress on the Riemann hypothesis, all produced by an internal model it has not released. A spokesperson told Scientific American that nearly every result came from a single prompt handed to a single agent — a sharp change from the 10,000-agent swarm, costing millions, behind its earlier Navier-Stokes claim. It lands on a field already in revolt over attribution: named mathematicians have accused the company of building on published work without citing it, and Quanta’s survey of the discipline this week found grief and deep distrust rather than celebration. (Scientific American, Quanta Magazine)

🌊 San Diego
  • Council punts on the water-rate overhaul — The City Council on Tuesday delayed a vote on scrapping San Diego’s tiered water rates in favour of a single unitary rate — the same price per gallon for everyone, regardless of how much they use or what kind of building they live in. The shift would lower bills for apartment buildings and raise them for most single-family homes. It is not a revenue increase; it is a reallocation, forced by courts that found the city never produced evidence justifying tiered pricing for single-family customers under Proposition 218, in litigation that began with a 2015 ratepayer suit. (KPBS)
  • Bankers Hill tenants win a billing fight — Tenants at an apartment building in Bankers Hill stopped paying their water, sewer and trash charges after their landlord replaced a flat $60 monthly utility fee with a ratio utility billing system, which splits building-wide costs by a formula — occupancy, square footage — rather than by what any apartment actually uses. One tenant’s bill hit $289 in a single month. They organised, demanded a line-by-line accounting, withheld payment, and the landlord dropped the system. (Voice of San Diego)
  • A councilmember floats weakening the mayor — At Politifest, Councilmember Henry Foster said he would consider bringing forward a ballot measure to repeal San Diego’s strong-mayor form of government, after reporting that Mayor Todd Gloria had privately described Foster’s defeat as his top political priority. “You would think his priority should be the people,” Foster said, pointing to infrastructure and the 2024 floods. (Voice of San Diego)

Broken coral stays viable for only about 45 days, and the permit covers a 10-day working window. (The Garden Island)
